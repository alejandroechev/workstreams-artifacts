The Riemann hypothesis / an interactive deep dive

The hidden
rhythm of primes.

Prime numbers look irregular. A function of a complex variable encodes that irregularity as a collection of waves. Riemann’s hypothesis says something extraordinarily precise about the size of every one of those waves.

ζ(ρ) = 0,  0 < Re(ρ) < 1   ⇒   Re(ρ) = ½

We will earn this sentence, not just memorize it. Start with calculus, algebra, and curiosity. No complex analysis or number theory assumed.

00 / The map

A statement about zeros.
A consequence about primes.

A prime is an integer greater than 1 divisible only by itself and 1. A zero of a function is an input where its output is zero. At first these have nothing to do with one another. The entire story is the bridge between them.

Integers → primesUnique factorization builds an infinite product.
Primes → ζ(s)The product equals a sum over all integers.
ζ(s) → zerosComplex analysis extends the function and reveals its zeros.
Zeros → errorEach zero contributes an oscillation to prime counting.

What you need coming in

Algebra; logarithms and exponentials; basic derivatives and integrals; the idea of a limit. We will develop convergence, complex numbers, analytic continuation, and asymptotic notation as we go.

The central mental model

A zero ρ = β + iγ contributes a term involving xβ cos(γ log x). β controls growth; γ controls oscillation. RH says every nontrivial zero has β = ½.

Epistemic ground rules. Theorem means proved; hypothesis means not proved. Every numerical experiment here is finite, approximate, and illustrative. None establishes RH. Hypothetical off-line zeros are explicitly labeled and never presented as discoveries.

01 / Arithmetic meets calculus

How many primes
are there up to x?

Write π(x) for the number of primes at most x. Here π is a counting function, not the circle constant. For example, π(10) = 4 because the primes are 2, 3, 5, 7. The graph is a staircase: every prime adds one step.

The prime number theorem (PNT), proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, says:

π(x) ∼ x / log x The symbol ∼ means that π(x) / (x / log x) tends to 1 as x → ∞. All logarithms in this lesson are natural.

Near a large x, a useful average prime density is 1/log x. Integrating a density gives a cumulative count, suggesting the more accurate smooth approximation Li(x) = ∫2x dt/log t. It is a heuristic motivation for an approximation, not a proof that primes are independently random.

PNT concerns relative accuracy; RH concerns the size of the remaining error. Knowing that an error is small compared with x/log x still leaves room for enormous absolute discrepancies. RH would give a much tighter, square-root-scale estimate, with logarithmic factors.

Calculus checkpoint: why Li(x), rather than just x/log x?

Differentiating x/log x gives 1/log x − 1/(log x)². It misses a term. Integration by parts gives

Li(x) = x/log x − 2/log 2 + ∫2x dt/(log t)².

Further integrations yield an asymptotic expansion beginning x/log x + x/(log x)² + 2x/(log x)³ + ⋯. The second term alone eventually dwarfs √x log x. So replacing Li(x) with x/log x in the RH error estimate would make the statement false.

Another common convention is li(x) = PV ∫0x dt/log t, where PV is a symmetric limiting procedure around the singularity at 1. Our Li(x) differs by the constant li(2) ≈ 1.04516. This constant does not affect the asymptotic RH statements.

02 / The language of oscillation

A complex exponent
is a shrinking, rotating arrow.

A complex number is s = σ + it, with i² = −1. Its real part σ is a horizontal coordinate; its imaginary part t is a vertical coordinate. Multiplication by eiθ rotates a vector through angle θ because eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ.

For a positive integer n, there is no logarithm-branch ambiguity: define n−s using the real log n. Then:

n−s = e−s log n = n−σ [cos(t log n) − i sin(t log n)].Length: n−σ. Angle: −t log n. Increasing σ shortens later arrows; increasing t changes their relative phases.

Adding complex numbers means adding arrows tip to tail. A sum can become small because the arrows cancel, even when none is small individually. This makes complex numbers a natural language for interference.

Experiment 02 / Make cancellation visible

Try t = 0: every term is positive real, so there is no cancellation. Then raise t. The path bends as differently sized arrows rotate through different angles. A zero of the continued zeta function will require both its real and imaginary parts to vanish, not merely one of them.

03 / A function built from all integers

Meet zeta. Respect
its domain.

ζ(s) = ∑n=1∞ n−s = 1 + 2−s + 3−s + ⋯,   Re(s) > 1.

An infinite sum is a limit of finite partial sums. It is not automatically a meaningful number. For this sum, |n−s| = n−σ, so the p-series test guarantees absolute convergence when σ > 1. Absolute convergence allows rearrangement and multiplication of these series without changing their values.

The integral test gives a concrete error estimate in this region:

|ζ(s) − ∑n=1N n−s| ≤ ∑n>N n−σ ≤ N1−σ / (σ − 1).A valid upper bound, not usually the actual error. It becomes expensive to approximate ζ by raw summation near σ = 1.

At s = 2, ζ(2) = π²/6 ≈ 1.644934. At s = 1, the series is the divergent harmonic series. In fact, the defining Dirichlet series does not converge anywhere with Re(s) ≤ 1. But the RH zeros are in 0 < Re(s) < 1. We therefore need a new representation of the same function—not a license to pretend divergent sums converge.

A small dose of complex analysis: holomorphic, pole, zero

A function is holomorphic on an open region if its complex derivative exists there. Unlike real differentiability, complex differentiability must give the same answer when approached from every direction. This is a very strong restriction: holomorphic functions locally equal convergent power series.

A pole is a controlled blow-up, like 1/(s−1). A function holomorphic except for isolated poles is meromorphic. A zero of multiplicity m has local form (s−ρ)mg(s), with g(ρ) ≠ 0. Consequently its logarithmic derivative f′/f behaves like m/(s−ρ), a fact we will use to extract primes from zeros.

A nonzero holomorphic function has isolated zeros; they cannot accumulate at an interior point. This does not forbid infinitely many zeros extending upward to unbounded imaginary height.

04 / The arithmetic bridge

Every integer is
a product of primes.

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says every integer n > 1 has a unique factorization n = p1a₁⋯pkaₖ. For each prime, a geometric series enumerates its powers:

(1 − p−s)−1 = 1 + p−s + p−2s + ⋯.
ζ(s) = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1,   Re(s) > 1.

When you multiply the factors, choose one power of each prime. Every resulting term is n−s, and unique factorization ensures each integer appears exactly once. Absolute convergence justifies passing from finite products to the infinite Euler product.

Thus ζ is not merely a function that happens to correlate with primes: its multiplication structure is made out of primes. The Euler product also implies ζ(s) ≠ 0 for Re(s) > 1. This needs the convergence of the product, not just the observation that each individual factor is nonzero: a limit of nonzero products can otherwise be zero.

The key derivative that exposes prime powers

For Re(s) > 1, take a locally consistent logarithm of the Euler product and use −log(1−z) = ∑k≥1 zk/k. Differentiate the absolutely convergent expansion:

log ζ(s) = ∑p ∑k≥1 p−ks/k,
−ζ′(s)/ζ(s) = ∑p ∑k≥1 (log p) p−ks = ∑n≥1 Λ(n)n−s.

The von Mangoldt function Λ(n) is log p if n = pk for a prime p and integer k ≥ 1, and 0 otherwise. For example, Λ(8) = log 2, Λ(9) = log 3, and Λ(12) = 0. This is why the cleanest explicit formula counts weighted prime powers rather than just primes.

05 / The necessary conceptual leap

The function is bigger
than its first formula.

Start with something familiar: 1 + z + z² + ⋯ = 1/(1−z), but only for |z| < 1. The expression on the right still defines a function at z = −2, where it equals 1/3. The series on the left diverges there.

Experiment 04 / Formula versus function

Leave the disk of convergence

Analytic continuation extends a holomorphic function through overlapping regions where other formulas make sense. The identity theorem supplies uniqueness: two holomorphic functions on a connected domain that agree on an open subset agree throughout that domain. You do not get to pick arbitrary new values.

For ζ, one route begins with the alternating eta series:

η(s) = 1 − 2−s + 3−s − 4−s + ⋯,
η(s) = (1 − 21−s) ζ(s).The identity is first derived for Re(s) > 1. The eta series converges for Re(s) > 0 and supplies a continuation into the critical strip.

Where the denominator is nonzero, ζ(s) = η(s)/(1−21−s). In the open strip 0 < Re(s) < 1 the denominator never vanishes. On Re(s) = 1 it vanishes at 1 + 2πik/log 2: the nonzero-k singularities of this quotient are removable, whereas s = 1 is the genuine pole. Other formulas continue ζ further.

The result is a unique meromorphic function on the entire complex plane, with one simple pole at s = 1 and residue 1: near 1, ζ(s) = 1/(s−1) + a holomorphic remainder.

Not ordinary summation. The continued value ζ(−1) = −1/12 does not mean the positive series 1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯ converges to a negative number. That series diverges. Analytic continuation is an extension of a function, not a redefinition of ordinary limits.
Why does the alternating series work even for complex s?

For complex inputs, the real alternating-series test alone is not enough. Instead pair terms: (2n−1)−s − (2n)−s = ∫2n−12n s u−s−1 du. On a compact subset of Re(s) > 0, these paired terms have a summable uniform bound proportional to n−1−δ for some δ > 0. The unpaired final term tends to zero.

This proves locally uniform convergence there, which preserves holomorphy. Splitting odd and even terms in the original absolutely convergent ζ series proves the eta identity for Re(s) > 1; uniqueness then gives the continuation.

06 / The geometry of the conjecture

Why the line
Re(s) = ½?

The functional equation links ζ(s) with ζ(1−s). One form is:

ζ(s) = 2s πs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1−s) ζ(1−s).Read as a meromorphic identity, using limits at apparent singularities. Here π is the circle constant.

The gamma function extends factorials: Γ(z) = ∫0∞ uz−1e−u du for Re(z) > 0, with Γ(n) = (n−1)!. It too has a meromorphic continuation. At s = −2, −4, −6, …, the sine factor vanishes while the other factors are finite and nonzero: these are the trivial zeros.

All other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1. The Euler product excludes the right-hand half-plane Re(s) > 1, a deeper theorem excludes Re(s) = 1, and the functional equation handles the reflected side. The statement that the line Re(s) = 1 is zero-free is already enough to prove the prime number theorem.

A cleaner symmetric object is the completed function:

ξ(s) = ½s(s−1)π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s),   ξ(s) = ξ(1−s).

The factors cancel the pole and the trivial zeros; ξ is entire (holomorphic everywhere) and its zeros are precisely the nontrivial zeros of ζ, with multiplicity. Also ζ(s̄) = ζ(s)̄, where the bar means complex conjugation. Thus a nontrivial zero β + iγ comes with reflections β − iγ, (1−β) + iγ, and (1−β) − iγ.

Experiment 05 / Symmetry is not a proof

A symmetric set need not sit on its axis

RH asserts that every nontrivial zero is on the symmetry axis itself. Reflection symmetry explains why ½ is special, but does not force any individual zero onto the line. Moving the hypothetical points off the line respects every displayed symmetry and violates RH.

Where does the functional equation come from?

A useful route is the theta function θ(t) = ∑n∈ℤ e−πn²t, for t > 0. The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is another Gaussian; Poisson summation gives θ(t) = t−1/2θ(1/t).

A Mellin transform (an integral against powers ts/2) gives, initially for Re(s) > 1:

π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s) = ½ ∫0∞ [θ(t)−1] ts/2−1 dt.

Split at t = 1 and use the theta transformation on the lower half. If Λζ(s) denotes the left side (not the von Mangoldt function), this becomes

Λζ(s) = −1/s + 1/(s−1)
+ ½ ∫1∞ [θ(t)−1] [ts/2−1 + t(1−s)/2−1] dt.

The integral converges for every complex s because θ(t)−1 decays exponentially. The right side is invariant under s ↔ 1−s. Multiplying by s(s−1)/2 removes its poles and produces ξ. This is a proof route for the symmetry—not a proof that all its zeros are central.

08 / The payoff

Zeros reconstruct
the prime staircase.

To make the bridge exact, replace π(x) with a weighted count of prime powers:

ψ(x) = ∑n≤x Λ(n) = ∑pk≤x log p.Each prime power pk contributes log p. At 2, 4, 8, 16, … the jump is always log 2, not log(pk).

At x = 10, ψ(10) = log 2 + log 3 + log 2 + log 5 + log 7 + log 2 + log 3 ≈ 7.832. It has jumps at 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9. The prime number theorem is equivalent to ψ(x) ∼ x.

The explicit formula expresses this arithmetic staircase in terms of the zeros:

ψ₀(x) = x − ∑ρ xρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1 − x−2),   x > 1.ψ₀ uses the midpoint of each jump. The zero sum includes multiplicities and means the symmetric limit over |Im ρ| ≤ T as T → ∞; it is not an arbitrarily reordered, absolutely convergent sum.

The pole at 1 generates the main term x. The nontrivial zeros generate the oscillatory error. The trivial zeros and the behavior at 0 generate the two smaller corrections. This is an exact theorem, not an assumption of RH.

For a conjugate pair ρ = β ± iγ, its combined contribution to the error is real:

−2 Re(xβ+iγ/(β+iγ))
= −2xβ [β cos(γ log x) + γ sin(γ log x)] / (β² + γ²).

Now the slogan is an equation: β controls amplitude growth; γ controls frequency in log x. Large γ adds finer detail; its coefficient is suppressed roughly by 1/γ. These are not ordinary equally spaced Fourier harmonics, but the reconstruction has a similar wave-superposition flavor.

Derivation sketch: the inverse transform and its residues

Recall −ζ′/ζ = ∑ Λ(n)n−s for Re(s) > 1. Perron inversion turns this encoding back into a count. For c > 1, with the appropriate symmetric limit,

ψ₀(x) = (1/(2πi)) ∫c−i∞c+i∞ [−ζ′(s)/ζ(s)] xs/s ds.

The kernel integrates (x/n)s/s to 1 when n < x, 0 when n > x, and ½ when n = x. That recovers the weighted count. To evaluate the integral, shift the vertical contour left. The residue theorem says the change equals 2πi times the sum of coefficients of (s−a)−1 at the crossed singularities.

  • At s = 1, ζ has a pole; −ζ′/ζ has residue +1, producing x.
  • At a zero ρ of multiplicity m, −ζ′/ζ has residue −m, producing −m xρ/ρ.
  • At s = 0, the factor 1/s contributes −ζ′(0)/ζ(0) = −log(2π).
  • The trivial zeros −2n contribute ∑n≥1 x−2n/(2n) = −½ log(1−x−2).

Controlling the horizontal and distant contour pieces and taking the symmetric limit requires estimates; the formal residue calculation alone is not the whole proof. But it exposes exactly why zeros govern arithmetic.

Why square-root-sized individual waves are not yet a proof of the RH error bound

Assuming RH makes each factor |xρ| = √x. But the sum over all zeros is not absolutely convergent; you cannot multiply √x by “the number of zeros.” One first truncates at height T and controls the remainder.

The number N(T) of zeros with 0 < Im ρ ≤ T, counting multiplicity, satisfies N(T) = (T/(2π))log(T/(2π)) − T/(2π) + O(log T). This implies ∑0<γ≤T 1/γ = O((log T)²). Bounding the truncated zero sum and treating the truncation carefully yields O(√x (log x)²). The logarithms encode real analytical work, not decorative corrections.

09 / What a proof would buy us

Control of the error.
Not a formula for everything.

The most direct consequence is also an equivalent formulation:

RH   ⇔   ψ(x) = x + O(√x (log x)²)
RH   ⇔   π(x) = Li(x) + O(√x log x).These are asymptotic statements as x → ∞. A proof of either estimate would prove RH, not merely follow from it.

Big O means there exist constants C and x₀ such that the absolute error is at most C times the displayed expression for every x ≥ x₀. It does not specify C, assert equality, or say the actual error always nearly attains the bound. The square-root exponent is the central feature; RH does not determine the sharpest possible logarithmic factors.

An equivalent exponent-focused version is ψ(x) = x + Oε(x1/2+ε) for every ε > 0, where the constant may depend on ε. By contrast PNT gives only ψ(x) = x + o(x), meaning (ψ(x)−x)/x → 0.

An off-line zero has a reflected partner with β > ½. Its growth factor exceeds √x by xβ−1/2, eventually beating any fixed power of log x. One cannot rule out cancellation just by staring at one term, but analytic arguments make the equivalence rigorous: the RH error estimates exclude zeros to the right of ½.

Why the error estimate conversely forces the zeros onto the line

For Re(s) > 1, partial summation gives −ζ′(s)/ζ(s) = s∫1∞ ψ(x)x−s−1 dx. Substitute ψ(x) = x + E(x):

−ζ′(s)/ζ(s) = s/(s−1) + s∫1∞ E(x)x−s−1 dx.

If E(x) = O(√x (log x)²), this integral defines a holomorphic function for Re(s) > ½. Apart from the known pole at 1, the right side has no poles there. A zero of ζ would create a pole of −ζ′/ζ, so no such zero can exist to the right of ½. Reflection then excludes zeros to the left of ½ inside the strip. This is the conceptual core of the reverse implication.

RH versus GRH. Dirichlet L-functions L(s,χ) = ∑ χ(n)n−s encode primes in residue classes, such as numbers congruent to a modulo q. The generalized Riemann hypothesis asserts analogous critical-line behavior for this larger family. Ordinary RH concerns ζ alone and does not prove GRH. Many widely quoted computational consequences actually assume GRH.

If RH were disproved: one rigorously certified nontrivial zero off the line would suffice. The equivalent RH error bounds would fail, but PNT and the explicit formula would remain true. A theorem of the form “if RH, then X” would remain a valid implication; its conclusion X would not automatically become false. Mathematics would not collapse—the conjectured precision would change.

10 / Evidence, not closure

Why isn’t this
already settled?

We have an exact function, exact symmetries, exact formulas tying it to primes, and enormous computational evidence. The missing step is a global reason that excludes every off-line zero at every height.

What is known Theorems

There are infinitely many nontrivial zeros; infinitely many lie on the critical line. All nontrivial zeros are in the open critical strip and have the reflection symmetries described above. Large finite height ranges have been rigorously checked.

What is missing Open

“Infinitely many on the line” is not “all on the line.” A very sparse infinite set of off-line zeros—or even one quartet—would defeat RH. The pole, the Euler product, and symmetry alone do not rule that out.

Three tempting, incomplete arguments

“The graph looks right.” A finite graph cannot establish an unbounded universal statement. Numerical plots can miss roots, and rounding near zero is not exact vanishing.

“The symmetry forces the zeros to the center.” Symmetric pairs off the center are just as symmetric. You constructed them in experiment 05.

“Prime randomness should imply square-root cancellation.” Randomness is a heuristic here, not a proved model of primes. Controlling all correlations with enough precision is the hard part, not something the analogy supplies for free.

Two research directions, and what they would need to prove

Spectral ideas (Hilbert–Pólya). If one could construct an appropriate self-adjoint operator whose eigenvalues correspond exactly to the numbers γ in zeros ½+iγ—and prove the correspondence captures all nontrivial zeros—then the reality of the spectrum would force the critical line. No such established construction currently proves RH. Similarity to quantum energy levels is inspiration, not a completed argument.

Positivity and equivalent criteria. RH has many reformulations as positivity conditions or arithmetic inequalities. These can move the difficulty to a more approachable-looking domain, but a universal positivity condition still needs a proof. Rephrasing the conjecture does not remove its content.

Analogous hypotheses have been proved for zeta functions associated with varieties over finite fields. Those successes motivate structural approaches, but do not by themselves prove the classical statement about the integers.

Status. RH is listed as unsolved by the Clay Mathematics Institute, with a US$1 million Millennium Prize attached under its prize rules. This lesson’s sources were consulted on 20 November 2026. A claimed proof is not the same thing as an accepted proof.

The one-minute version you should now be able to give

Unique factorization makes the zeta function an Euler product over primes. Analytic continuation extends it into the critical strip, where it has infinitely many zeros. A contour-integral formula expresses the error in a weighted prime count as a sum of contributions xρ/ρ from those zeros. The real part of each ρ controls growth. RH says all these real parts are ½, which is equivalent to near-square-root control of the prime-counting error. Symmetry and finite computations support the picture but do not prove the universal claim.

Numerical methods, limits, and reproducibility

Prime counts and ψ are computed directly with a sieve up to 10,000. Li(x) is evaluated as Ei(log x)−Ei(log 2), using the convergent expansion Ei(u) = γE + log u + ∑k≥1 uk/(k·k!) for u > 0. Here γE is Euler’s constant, not a zero height.

The zeta probe uses Euler–Maclaurin summation with N = 64 and eight Bernoulli correction terms. It is restricted to 0.1 ≤ σ ≤ 0.9 and 10 ≤ t ≤ 50, a modest window where this truncation is accurate. The browser uses ordinary double-precision arithmetic, not interval arithmetic. Values shown as small are approximate, not certificates.

The 60 positive zero heights are embedded rounded reference values computed using mpmath at 25 decimal-digit working precision. The reconstruction pairs each with its complex conjugate. No uncomputed zeros are assumed absent. A finite truncation can ring, overshoot, and perform unevenly near prime powers.
